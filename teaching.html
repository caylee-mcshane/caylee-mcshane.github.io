Adjunct Professor · CSU San Marcos

Machine learning and AI for biotechnologists, taught with ethics at the center.

I Machine Learning & AI in the Biotechnology M.S. program at California State University San Marcos — a graduate course spanning data science, deep learning, large language models, and AI ethics. It gives working scientists practical fluency with these tools and the judgment to apply them responsibly in a field where the stakes are human health.

The course

The students are graduate biotechnologists, not computer scientists, and the course meets them there. Lectures, hands-on Python notebooks, and case studies drawn from the life sciences build both real capability with the tools and a critical sense for where they fail quietly. It's taught in person with separate laboratory and lectures components

Curriculum

The course moves from data-science foundations through modern deep learning and large language models, with applied biotechnology projects along the way and ethics woven into every stage.

  1. Foundations of data science

    Types of learning, the evolution of ML, and the languages, tools, and notebooks used in practice.

  2. Statistics & exploratory data analysis

    Descriptive and inferential statistics, data collection and cleaning, sampling, and guarding against bias.

  3. Supervised learning

    Regression and classification with logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, SVMs, and KNN.

  4. Model evaluation & tuning

    Train/test splits, cross-validation, hyperparameter tuning, and metrics such as accuracy, F1, and ROC.

  5. Unsupervised learning

    Clustering with K-means and hierarchical methods, and where it applies in the life sciences.

  6. Deep learning

    Neural-network fundamentals: perceptrons, backpropagation, activation functions, CNNs, and RNNs.

  7. Generative AI & agents

    Generative models, agentic architectures, and applications such as AI agents in drug discovery.

  8. Large language models

    Transformers, attention, tokenization, and how modern LLMs represent and generate language.

Ethics in the curriculum

Ethics isn't a single lecture bolted on at the end. It runs through the course, because the scientists in the room will decide how these systems get used.

Students examine algorithmic bias and fairness, surveillance and privacy, and the societal impact of automated decisions — then turn to the dual-use questions that are especially acute in biotechnology, where the same capabilities that speed discovery can also cause harm.

The throughline is responsible deployment: how to reason about AI safety and oversight, and about the limits of trusting a confident-sounding model. It's the same set of questions that drives my research, which makes the course a natural place to introduce them.
