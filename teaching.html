I teach [course name / number] in the Biotechnology M.S. program at California State University San Marcos, a course I designed from the ground up. It gives graduate scientists working fluency with modern machine learning and the judgment to apply it responsibly in a field where the stakes are human health.

The course

The students are practicing and aspiring biotechnologists, not computer scientists. The course meets them there: it builds intuition for how machine learning and LLM systems actually work, where they succeed, and — just as important — where they fail quietly. The goal is twofold: practical capability with the tools, and a critical framework for deciding when not to trust them.

Curriculum

I built the syllabus, materials, and assessments for the course. The arc moves from foundations to applied practice, with ethics integrated at every stage rather than saved for the end.

  1. Foundations of machine learning

    How models learn from data; core model families; what "training" really means.

  2. Data, bias, and validity

    Where data comes from, how bias enters, and how to tell a real result from an artifact.

  4. Applying ML in biotechnology

    Case studies in the life sciences; matching methods to problems; avoiding overreach.

  5. Ethics and responsible deployment

    Consent, fairness, oversight, dual-use, and safety — carried through the whole course.

Ethics in the curriculum

Ethics isn't a single lecture bolted on at the end. It runs through every module, because the scientists in the room will be the ones deciding how these systems get used.

Because these students will carry ML into clinical and biological settings, the ethical questions are not abstract. We work through where training data comes from and what consent means for it; how bias in data becomes bias in decisions that affect patients; why interpretability matters and how overtrust in a confident-sounding model causes harm; and the dual-use questions specific to biotechnology, where the same capability can help or endanger.

The course closes with an introduction to AI safety and alignment: why making capable systems behave as intended is an open problem, how the field thinks about oversight and control, and what responsible deployment asks of the people building and applying these tools. It's the throughline that connects my teaching to my research.
